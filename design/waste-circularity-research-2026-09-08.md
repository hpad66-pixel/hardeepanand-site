## Miami-Dade status and chronology

- February 12, 2023: fire disabled the Doral Resources Recovery Facility. The county still lists it as non-operational. [County facility status](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/service.page?Mduid_service=ser150282068351856), [county capacity record identifying the date](https://doral.legistar.com/View.ashx?GUID=37632CD6-4013-45AC-A50B-F845825943C8&ID=14237084&M=F).
- Former capacity: approximately one million tons annually, reiterated in the county's March 20, 2026 planning task-force minutes. [County meeting record](https://www.miamidade.gov/resources/economy/planning/documents/2026-03-20-minutes.pdf).
- January 2025 county memo documents 3.5 million tons of contracted landfill capacity, with truck and rail transport. This is contracted capacity, not measured annual shipments. Its four-to-five-year local landfill-life estimates are dated projections. [County memo](https://www.miamidade.gov/solidwaste/library/2025-01-25-second-update-sustainable-solid-waste-campus-directive-241676.pdf).
- July 14 reporting, updated July 15, 2026: proposed replacement capital cost approximately $3.16 billion; mayor recommended pausing consortium negotiations. FPL disputed the framing of the cost comparison. This is a proposal estimate, not an approved budget or expenditure. [Local 10](https://www.local10.com/news/local/2026/07/14/cost-of-new-miami-dade-incinerator-balloons-to-eye-popping-316-billion-mayor-says/).
- July 31, 2026: county unveiled a Zero Waste Master Plan with 31 strategies and reported countywide generation exceeding five million tons annually. A plan launch is not demonstrated diversion. [County announcement](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/release.page?Mduid_release=rel1785524734267603).
- August 6, 2026 coverage discusses recycling, organics infrastructure, food recovery, and hauler reporting. [Waste Dive](https://www.wastedive.com/news/miami-dade-zero-waste-plan-2040/827206/).

No later confirmed operational replacement was located. Do not describe a recommended location, target opening, proposed contract, or planning target as finalized. Recheck before publication.

## Mechanisms and decision implications

- Sediment from construction runoff harms aquatic habitat. Soil, dust, concrete washwater, and hauled debris need separate controls and measurements. [EPA construction stormwater guidance](https://www.epa.gov/npdes/national-menu-best-management-practices-bmps-stormwater-construction), [concrete washout guidance](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2020-02/documents/concretewashout.pdf).
- EPA ranks food prevention, donation, and upcycling highly. Direct delivery to a digester is distinct from sending food through sewers. Its food scale evaluates environmental pathways, not complete project economics. [EPA Wasted Food Scale](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/wasted-food-scale).
- Combustion reduces disposal volume and recovers energy but leaves ash. Net climate comparison needs transport, fossil CO2, other emissions, displaced electricity, and recovered metals. [EPA combustion overview](https://www.epa.gov/smm/energy-recovery-combustion-municipal-solid-waste-msw), [EPA WARM v16 management documentation](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-01/warm_management_practices_v16_dec.pdf).

## Editorial synthesis, proposed by the authoring assistant

Working title: **There Is No Away**. Subtitle: **What a burned waste plant, a demolition site, and an uneaten meal reveal about the decisions we make.**

Start at Doral: one facility stopped, but the material flow continued. Then distinguish construction's material burden from food's methane burden. Follow the upstream water, energy, land, and procurement commitments. Bring the argument back to investment: characterize the stream, prevent avoidable losses, establish real recovery destinations, size residual capacity using credible scenarios, and retain contingencies for disruptions and slower-than-planned diversion.

Leadership outcomes: remaining permitted disposal capacity and service reliability; virgin materials actually displaced; net lifecycle emissions; water withdrawals avoided; lifetime cost; local exposure; accountable delivery milestones. Tonnage alone cannot stand in for all of them.

An exact incremental climate footprint of the Doral outage has NOT been established by this research. It requires actual destination-specific shipments, transport modes/distances, landfill gas performance, waste composition, displaced electricity, and a consistent baseline. National factors cannot substitute for those records. No claim is made that climate change caused the fire.
