The plant stopped. The garbage still had to be collected.

On February 12, 2023, a fire disabled the Resources Recovery Facility in Doral. More than three years later, Miami-Dade County still lists it as non-operational. The facility had roughly one million tons of annual processing capacity. That is a large service obligation to find another home for. [County facility status](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/service.page?Mduid_service=ser150282068351856); [county capacity record](https://doral.legistar.com/View.ashx?GUID=37632CD6-4013-45AC-A50B-F845825943C8&ID=14237084&M=F); [March 2026 planning minutes](https://www.miamidade.gov/resources/economy/planning/documents/2026-03-20-minutes.pdf).

I have spent much of my career inside public infrastructure. This is the part of the story I pay attention to: an asset can stop working while the obligation it serves continues without interruption.

The homes are still occupied. The restaurants still serve dinner. The construction crews still fill the next container. Someone has to find capacity, arrange transport, protect public health, and keep the service moving.

From the curb, the service can look unchanged. Inside the system, the work has changed completely.

## The cost finds another address

Following the fire, the county relied on contracted disposal capacity, including transport by truck and rail. A January 2025 county memo documents agreements covering 3.5 million tons of landfill capacity. That is contracted capacity, not a claim that all of it was used. [County disposal memo](https://www.miamidade.gov/solidwaste/library/2025-01-25-second-update-sustainable-solid-waste-campus-directive-241676.pdf).

By July 2026, the debate over a replacement had reached a proposed capital cost of approximately $3.16 billion. The mayor recommended pausing negotiations; FPL disputed how the comparison with earlier estimates was being presented. That figure is a proposal estimate, not an approved budget or money already spent. [Local 10, updated July 15, 2026](https://www.local10.com/news/local/2026/07/14/cost-of-new-miami-dade-incinerator-balloons-to-eye-popping-316-billion-mayor-says/).

Then, on July 31, the county unveiled a Zero Waste Master Plan with 31 strategies. Its announcement reported more than five million tons of waste generated countywide each year. That broad total is different from the former plant's capacity and the volume handled by county collection crews. [Miami-Dade announcement](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/release.page?Mduid_release=rel1785524734267603).

<aside class="essay-status-note"><strong>Status checked September 8, 2026.</strong> The Doral facility remains listed as non-operational. Replacement costs and plans described here are proposals; the waste-reduction plan's results remain to be demonstrated.</aside>

There are two decisions here that need to be made together. How much disposal capacity will the county need? And how much material can it credibly prevent, retain, reuse, or recover before that capacity is built?

Build as though nothing will change and you may commit to capacity the system will not need. Assume every reduction target will be achieved on schedule and you may leave the people running collection with nowhere to take the material.

Both errors can be expensive. The answer needs scenarios, operating evidence, and a contingency. A target alone cannot collect tomorrow's garbage.

## Before the dumpster, there was a drawing

Look at a demolition site. The material in the container was once a specification, a purchase, and an asset somebody intended to use.

By the time the hauling contractor arrives, many of the choices that determine what can be saved have already been made.

This covers buildings, roads, bridges, and other structures, not just new construction. It is a dated national estimate, not a measurement of this year's building activity. [EPA construction and demolition data](https://www.epa.gov/smm/sustainable-management-construction-and-demolition-materials).

Keeping a usable structure and crushing its concrete into road base are different achievements. Both deserve evaluation. A diversion percentage alone cannot tell a board which preserved more useful life or avoided more new production.

Before authorizing demolition, I would want to see the comparison: retain and retrofit, selectively dismantle, or demolish and replace. Include the condition of the asset, the service it must provide, safety, operating performance, cost, and the realistic destination of recovered material.

Then there is the dirt that never reaches a container. Exposed soil can leave a construction site in runoff. EPA describes how sediment can reduce light reaching aquatic plants and damage aquatic habitat. Concrete washwater requires separate containment and management. The material leaving through the gate and the material leaving through the drain belong in the same project review. [EPA construction runoff guidance](https://www.epa.gov/npdes/national-menu-best-management-practices-bmps-stormwater-construction); [concrete washout guidance](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2020-02/documents/concretewashout.pdf).

UNEP's 2025–2026 report places the broad buildings and construction sector at around **37 percent of global CO₂ emissions**. That includes building operations; it is not a percentage attributable to demolition debris. The point is to evaluate the whole life of what we build, including the consequences of replacing it. [UNEP report](https://www.unep.org/resources/report/global-status-report-buildings-and-construction-2025-2026).

## A ton is a weight, not a diagnosis

A ton of concrete and a ton of discarded food can occupy the same column in a disposal report. Their consequences are different.

ReFED estimates that **60 million tons of food, about 25 percent of the U.S. food supply, reached waste destinations in 2024**. Those destinations include landfills, incineration, sewers, and food left in fields. We should not describe all of it as landfilled. [ReFED's national overview](https://refed.org/food-waste/the-problem).

The organization's 2026 report estimates the value of that food waste at **$325 billion**. For the broader category of surplus food, which includes some donated food and recovered uses, it estimates **219 million metric tons of CO₂-equivalent emissions** across the lifecycle and **16 trillion gallons of water** used to grow it. These are modeled 2024 estimates, not meter readings or municipal disposal bills. [ReFED 2026 report, pages 8–9](https://refed.org/downloads/food-waste-report-design-final-2026.pdf).

That water number brings this straight into my world.

By the time food reaches the bin, water has already been used to grow it. Energy and labor have already gone into producing and moving it. A better collection route can improve the last part of the process. Prevention reaches further back.

And disposal adds a consequence of its own. Food decomposing without oxygen in a landfill generates methane. EPA estimates that food accounts for **58 percent of the methane escaping from U.S. municipal solid-waste landfills**. Its rapid decay can begin before gas collection is installed or expanded. [EPA landfill methane analysis](https://www.epa.gov/land-research/quantifying-methane-emissions-landfilled-food-waste).

If your capital plan treats every diverted ton as an equivalent result, ask what the metric is leaving out.

## The bin starts the investigation

There is a temptation to turn all this into a verdict on people's character. I think that makes the analysis weaker.

Discarded food could point to purchasing, forecasting, storage, preparation, or portioning. A scrapped pump could point to damage, missing parts, obsolete controls, or a replacement that genuinely performs better. The waste tells you where to look. You still have to establish the cause.

This is what I mean by a design audit. Follow one recurring loss back through the decisions that produced it. Find the specification, incentive, handoff, or operating constraint that can actually be changed.

Imagine procurement selecting equipment on initial price while maintenance carries the cost of inaccessible components for the next twenty years. The purchase can meet its target while the system pays more.

The question I would ask is simple: did we lower the cost, or move it into someone else's budget?

<blockquote class="pullquote"><span class="pq-mark" aria-hidden="true">“</span><p>The waste tells you where to look. You still have to establish the cause.</p><cite class="pq-cite"><span class="pq-name">Hardeep Anand</span><span class="pq-publication">The Systems Lens</span></cite></blockquote>

Circularity becomes useful when it changes that decision. Keep an asset working where that makes sense. Avoid ordering material that will never be used. Design components so they can be maintained or recovered. Establish a real next user before calling an output a resource.

That last step matters. Recoverable material still needs a customer, an acceptable quality, a route to market, and someone responsible for the connection.

## Close the loop that actually works

EPA's Wasted Food Scale puts prevention, donation, and upcycling among the most environmentally preferred pathways. For food that remains, composting or appropriately managed anaerobic digestion can recover useful resources. The scale evaluates environmental pathways; a project still needs its own operating and financial case. [EPA Wasted Food Scale](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/wasted-food-scale).

Water resource recovery facilities can be part of that conversation. EPA distinguishes food delivered directly to a digester from food sent through the sewer. Those are different pathways, with different consequences. A garbage disposal in the kitchen is not a substitute for a designed organics recovery system. [EPA pathway definitions](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/wasted-food-scale).

Before a utility accepts another organic feedstock, I would ask about contamination, loading, receiving facilities, staffing, gas handling, residuals, and dependable destinations for what comes out. A promising energy balance does not answer all those questions.

The same discipline belongs in the waste-to-energy discussion. Combustion can recover energy and reduce the volume needing disposal, but it leaves ash. A net climate comparison must consider transport, combustion emissions, landfill methane, recovered materials, and the electricity displaced. [EPA combustion overview](https://www.epa.gov/smm/energy-recovery-combustion-municipal-solid-waste-msw); [EPA lifecycle methodology](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-01/warm_management_practices_v16_dec.pdf).

The Doral fire does not establish which replacement option has the lowest overall impact. That requires local operating data and a fair comparison. I would not put an additional carbon number on the outage without actual shipments, destinations, transport modes, waste composition, landfill performance, and a consistent baseline.

Showing the limits of the evidence is part of showing the work.

## Make the next decision differently

In [The Civic Curriculum We Never Had](/writing/civic-curriculum/), I asked whether people understand the systems they depend on. In [Your Capital Plan Needs a Glucose Monitor](/writing/vital-signs/), I asked whether we can show that an investment improved the system.

This is the next question: what future costs and dependencies are we putting into the system when we approve the investment?

A region needs reliable disposal capacity. It also needs to avoid buying, producing, and discarding material unnecessarily. The people responsible for those outcomes should be working from the same forecast, with the same assumptions visible.

Start with one material stream and one decision you can change. Put the operator, the budget owner, and the person writing the specification in the same room. Establish the baseline. Name the intended result. Come back with the evidence.

Convenience is worth protecting. Safe water at the tap and dependable collection at the curb are achievements. They depend on work that should remain visible to the people funding and designing the system.

The truck leaving your street is the beginning of another responsibility. Follow it far enough and you find the next asset, the next operating budget, the next watershed, and the next community.

There is no away. There is a next destination, and we have a say in how much needs to go there.

<section class="article-takeaways" id="takeaways" aria-labelledby="takeaways-title">
<h2 id="takeaways-title">What to take into your next capital review</h2>
<p class="takeaways-intro">Five decisions that connect waste reduction with reliable infrastructure.</p>
<ol class="takeaways-list">
<li><strong>Size the system against more than one future.</strong> Require forecasts with credible prevention and recovery, slower implementation, growth, and major outages. Identify dependable capacity for the material that remains in each scenario.</li>
<li><strong>Review retention before authorizing replacement.</strong> Compare keeping and upgrading the asset with dismantling or replacing it. Include service requirements, safety, maintenance, energy, recoverable components, and lifetime cost.</li>
<li><strong>Prioritize by consequence, not tonnage alone.</strong> Track the material, its destination, net lifecycle emissions, water implications, local impacts, and actual displacement of new resources. Give food and construction debris different interventions.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm the next user and assign the handoff.</strong> Before financing recovery, establish the output specification, receiving capacity, transport, customer commitment, and responsibility when quality or demand changes.</li>
<li><strong>Close the financial and operating loop.</strong> Identify who pays, who benefits, and who owns the result. Set a baseline and review date so measured outcomes change the next specification, contract, or investment.</li>
</ol>
<p class="takeaways-question"><strong>One question to ask:</strong> Which future costs and dependencies are we designing into this investment, and what evidence would justify a different choice?</p>
