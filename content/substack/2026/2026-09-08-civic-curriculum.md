You should be able to graduate from high school knowing where your drinking water comes from.

The actual source. The people who treat it. What it takes to get it to your kitchen. Where it goes after you pull the plug.

Then you should be able to open a water bill and understand what you are paying for.

That would be a useful civics lesson.

We teach the branches of government. We teach elections, rights, and how a bill becomes law. Those things matter. Knowing your rights is part of knowing when power has overstepped.

But somewhere between the diagram of government and the experience of living under it, we leave a large gap.

Who maintains the pipe under your street? Who decides when to replace it? Where does the money come from? What happens when the replacement gets postponed, and who carries the consequence?

The bill becomes law. Fine.

How does the water become safe?

That is where I would start.

## The strange reward for getting it right

I spent much of my career inside water infrastructure. Even so, I turn on the tap and take it for granted like everyone else.

I wrote about this in [Fifty Steps Back, Five Hundred Steps Forward](/writing/fifty-steps-back/): reliable systems earn our inattention. The water arrives, so the work disappears.

The operator. The laboratory. The maintenance crew. The electricity. The watershed. The budget decision made years before you moved into the neighborhood.

All of it is present in that glass. Almost none of it is visible.

Then a main breaks or a bill goes up, and the system enters public life through the one thing nobody can ignore: a problem.

Now we are trying to understand decades of decisions in a public meeting with three minutes at the microphone.

That is a difficult place to begin an education.

I want us to begin earlier.

## Teach the connections

Systems literacy sounds like something you need a certificate to discuss. It starts with ordinary questions.

Where does this come from? What does it depend on? Who looks after it? Who pays? What happens next?

Follow your water far enough and you reach electricity. Follow the electricity and you reach fuel, transmission, land, and investment. Follow a new housing development and you reach water capacity, sewer capacity, roads, schools, and decisions about who funds the additions.

The connections are already there. Our education should make them visible.

This is the same problem I keep returning to inside utilities. People hold different pieces of the picture. Operations knows one part. Finance knows another. Planning has a third. The resident sees the bill and the construction outside the house.

Each sees something real. The trouble begins when any one piece is treated as the whole.

A useful civic education would teach us to ask what sits outside our view.

You do not need to become a treatment engineer to participate in a conversation about water. You need enough understanding to ask a useful question, recognize an incomplete answer, and know whose knowledge is missing.

## Rethink. Reduce. Rewire.

These three words are useful only if they change what we do.

**Rethink what counts as success.**

Picture a discussion about a proposed water-rate increase. Keeping the bill affordable is a legitimate concern. So is maintaining the system that delivers the water.

The conversation needs both.

What work would the increase fund? What evidence supports it? What happens if we postpone it? What help is available to households already struggling?

Understanding the system does not settle the disagreement. It gives the disagreement something concrete to work with.

**Reduce what makes the system harder to understand and maintain.**

Waste includes leaking water. It also includes asking residents to repeat the same report to three departments because nobody owns the handoff.

But “reduce” requires judgment. A standby pump may look unnecessary until the duty pump fails. Maintenance can look like an easy saving until the repair becomes an emergency.

Teach people to distinguish waste from protection. Otherwise, efficiency becomes a pleasant word for removing the things we will need later.

**Rewire the connection between a problem and a response.**

A resident reports recurring flooding. Someone records it. Someone investigates. A decision follows. The resident can see what happened, and the next storm helps show whether the response worked.

That is a feedback loop in everyday language.

In [Your Capital Plan Needs a Glucose Monitor](/writing/vital-signs/), I asked whether we could show that an investment improved the system. The civic version belongs beside it:

<blockquote class="pullquote"><span class="pq-mark" aria-hidden="true">“</span><p>When a community tells us something is wrong, can we show what happened next?</p><cite class="pq-cite"><span class="pq-name">Hardeep Anand</span><span class="pq-publication">The Systems Lens</span></cite></blockquote>

## Eight lessons, with the town as the classroom

I would keep the curriculum practical. Every lesson should leave the learner able to do something they could not do before.

<figure class="editorial-sketch" id="learn-from-the-operator">

</figure>

**1. Systems thinking: follow a consequence.**

Take a proposed development. Trace its possible effects on water demand, drainage, traffic, and school capacity. Ask which effects appear immediately and which take years. Mark what you know and what you still need to find out.

**2. The civic stack: find the decision.**

Choose one service and identify who operates it, who funds it, and who oversees it. These may be different organizations. Learn where the relevant budget and meeting records live. Find the point where a resident can participate.

**3. Digital literacy and information hygiene: check the claim.**

Take a claim about local water quality. Find its original source, date, location, and what was actually measured. Practice saying, “This does not tell us enough yet.” Apply the same standard to an AI answer. A fluent explanation still needs evidence.

**4. Resource feedback loops: read your own record.**

Use a water bill to understand consumption over time. Separate the amount used from the amount charged. Ask what might explain a change and how you would check. Learn the difference between spotting a pattern and knowing its cause.

**5. Interdependence and infrastructure: follow a disruption.**

Consider a power outage at a pumping station. What depends on that station? What backup arrangements exist? Who needs to communicate with whom? Use public information and ask the utility to explain preparedness in terms residents can understand.

**6. Policy mapping and local advocacy: make a useful contribution.**

Read one proposal before the meeting. Identify the decision, the evidence, and the unanswered question. Submit a specific comment. Learn how to follow the response afterward. Participation includes the work between meetings.

**7. Conscious consumption: follow the cost beyond the receipt.**

Choose an everyday purchase and investigate the water, energy, materials, and disposal it involves. Recognize the limits of individual choice too. A household cannot choose a service or facility that its community does not provide.

**8. Ethics, empathy, and judgment: hold two obligations together.**

Work through a water system that needs investment while some residents cannot afford higher bills. Listen to the operator and the household. Examine the options, who benefits, and who bears the burden. Technical understanding should make us more attentive to people.

The final assignment could fit on one page: explain one local service, identify one unresolved question, and show where an answer or decision should come from.

I would find that more useful than another set of definitions remembered until Friday.

## Institutions have homework too

There is a limit to asking citizens to become better informed while making information difficult to use.

A budget can be publicly available and still be nearly impossible to understand. A complaint can receive a tracking number and still disappear from the resident’s point of view.

If we ask people to learn the system, the institutions running it need to help explain it.

Show what the money is intended to improve. Explain the tradeoffs. Make responsibility visible. Say what remains uncertain. Report what happened after the decision.

And respect the people doing the work. An operator should not have to defend an entire institution every time someone asks about a service failure. Residents should be able to question a decision without being dismissed as people who do not understand the technical details.

Trust grows when both sides can examine the same problem.

Understanding also takes time. Someone working two jobs should still receive safe water and accountable government. Civic education should make participation easier, while public institutions continue carrying their responsibilities.

## Start with one system

I do not think a curriculum will resolve every source of mistrust or fix every broken institution.

I do think we can give people a better starting point.

A school can build a lesson around the local water system. A library can host a conversation with an operator and someone who understands the budget. A utility board can explain one investment through the result it is supposed to produce, then return with the evidence.

Start small enough that the connection can be followed from beginning to end.

Where does our water come from?

What keeps it safe?

Who makes the decisions?

How will we know whether those decisions worked?

Those questions belong in the same education as rights, representation, and voting. They connect public power to daily life.

Rethink what we teach.

Reduce the distance between the institution and the people it serves.

Rewire the lesson so that understanding leads to participation, and participation receives a response.

Start with the tap. Follow it together.

<section class="article-takeaways" id="takeaways" aria-labelledby="takeaways-title">
<h2 id="takeaways-title">What to take into your community</h2>
<p class="takeaways-intro">Five ways to turn systems literacy into something you can practice.</p>
<ol class="takeaways-list">
<li><strong>Trace one service you depend on.</strong> Start with your water. Identify its source, the organization that delivers it, and where it goes after use. Write down what you cannot yet explain.</li>
<li><strong>Connect the bill to a decision.</strong> Find one proposed investment. Ask what it should improve, what happens if it is postponed, and how affordability is being addressed.</li>
<li><strong>Protect what prevents failure.</strong> Before calling something waste, ask what risk it manages. A standby pump and preventive maintenance need to be judged by the protection they provide.</li>
<li><strong>Follow a report through to the result.</strong> Ask who owns the response, when the next update is due, and what evidence will show whether conditions improved. Use the result to inform the next decision.</li>
<li><strong>Bring the people holding different pieces together.</strong> Ask a school or library to host a conversation with an operator, a budget lead, and residents. Leave with a shared explanation and one question to follow up.</li>
</ol>
<p class="takeaways-question"><strong>One question to ask:</strong> Can we follow one public problem from the first report to the decision, the result, and the next action?</p>
